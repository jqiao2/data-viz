Ikon El Niño Outlook

How each of the 77 ski areas on the Ikon Base Pass has fared in El Niño winters since 1950, and what that implies for the very strong event building now.

Bottom line

A very strong El Niño shifts the winter storm track south. Over 76 winters of reanalysis data that has meant more snow for Southern California, northern New Mexico and the western Alps, and less for the Pacific Northwest, interior British Columbia, the Canadian Rockies, Montana and the Great Lakes. The effect is real but modest: even at the best-placed resort, El Niño explains about 15% of the winter-to-winter swing.

Best odds in North America and Europe

  • Big Bear Mountain Resort80%predicted +40% · unlimited on Base

    Strongest and most reliable El Niño link of all 77 resorts. Beat normal in 7 of the 9 strong El Niño winters, with a median of +54%. Real records suggest the model overstates the link a little; nearer 70% is a fair read.

  • Snow Valley79%predicted +38% · unlimited on Base

    Same storms, same story as Big Bear, 15 km west. Unlimited on Base.

  • Taos Ski Valley76%predicted +19% · 5 days on Base

    Smaller gain and the steadiest record, but the least certain pick: the model tracks Taos records poorly (see Validation).

  • Chamonix Mont-Blanc Valley76%predicted +15% · 5 days on Base

    The western Alps lean snowy in El Niño winters. Valle d'Aosta, Megève and Zermatt show the same tilt.

Weakest odds

  • The Highlands30%predicted -12% · 5 days on Base

    Michigan and Minnesota resorts (The Highlands, Boyne, Lutsen) won only 2–4 of 9 strong El Niño winters.

  • Schweitzer32%predicted -11% · 5 days on Base

    Northern Idaho and the Washington Cascades (Snoqualmie, Crystal) lose about 8–11% per degree of ONI.

  • Sun Peaks Resort34%predicted -7% · 5 days on Base

    Interior BC and the SkiBig3 resorts lean dry. Lake Louise and Banff Sunshine beat normal in only 2 of 9.

  • Grandvalira Resorts46%predicted -3% · 5 days on Base

    The only European resort below even odds. The Pyrenees sit south of the storm track the Alps benefit from.

Tahoe and Mammoth

The straight-line fit puts them near even odds because weak and moderate El Niños do little there. In strong ones they do better: Palisades and Sierra-at-Tahoe beat normal in 7 of 9, Mammoth in 5 of 9 in the model. Mammoth's own 57-year record does better: above average in 6 of its 7 strong El Niño winters. Treat them as better bets than the table says.

Southern Hemisphere, 2026 season

The El Niño season is already here and nearly over. Valle Nevado has the strongest link of any resort (r = +0.52) and is at +14% so far. Thredbo (-45%) and Mt Buller (-43%) are having the poor season Australian history predicts. The New Zealand resorts are running 26–30% below normal despite a slightly positive history.

How to read the tables

Chance above normal is the probability this winter beats the resort's 1991–2020 average, from a line fitted through every winter since 1950–51 against its peak ONI and evaluated at +2.16. Predicted is that line's departure; the range bar shows ±1 standard deviation of past winters around the line (about two winters in three fall inside it; axis −60% to +110%). r measures how tightly snowfall follows ENSO. Strong El Niño record counts strong and very strong winters (peak ONI ≥ 1.5) that beat normal. Select a resort name for its season-by-season chart.

What matters is whether the model gets the good and bad seasons right. California holds up

Palisades, Mammoth, Snow Valley and Big Bear track at r = 0.72–0.95, so the El Niño tilt there comes from real weather. Two refinements: Big Bear's NOAA station shows a weaker ENSO link than the model (+0.25 vs +0.35), and Mammoth's own record beats average in more strong El Niño winters than the model does (6 of 7 vs 4 of 7).

Taos and the Alps are uncertain

Taos tracks at only r = 0.32–0.33 against both its OnTheSnow totals and the nearest long NOAA record. The Alps series cover only 12–13 seasons of self-reported OnTheSnow totals with two strong El Niño winters, so they neither confirm nor rule out the European tilt.

Method and limits

  • Snowfall is modelled, not measured. It comes from ERA5 hourly reanalysis (Copernicus) at the grid point nearest each resort, 1950 to the present. ERA5's terrain is smoothed to about 28 km and often sits several hundred to over 1,000 m below the resort, so temperature is adjusted to mid-mountain at 6.5°C per km. Precipitation counts as snow below 0.5°C and rain above 2°C, at 7:1 snow to water. Totals will not match resort reports; departures from each resort's own normal are the meaningful numbers.
  • El Niño strength is the highest NOAA ONI reading in Nov–Mar for northern resorts and May–Sep for southern ones. The outlook uses the latest reading, +2.16 for Jul–Sep. Events usually peak in early winter, so a higher peak would push every tilt slightly further.
  • Chance above normal assumes winters scatter evenly around the fitted line. It ignores long-term trends, which flatters resorts that have been snowier lately (Yunding) and penalises ones that have dried out.
  • Samples are small. There have been 9 strong or very strong El Niño winters since 1950. The fitted line uses all 76 complete winters, but an r below about 0.23 is within the range chance alone produces.
  • Resort list and coordinates come from ikonpass.com (2026–27 Base Pass) and public sources. Multi-mountain areas (Dolomiti Superski, Valle d'Aosta, Niseko United) use one representative point.
